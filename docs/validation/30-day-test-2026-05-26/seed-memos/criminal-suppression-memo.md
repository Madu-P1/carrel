**Re:** Motion to suppress — warrantless stop, search, and statements of defendant Okafor

## Question Presented

Whether physical evidence and statements obtained after officers stopped, frisked, searched, and then interrogated the defendant without a warrant must be suppressed.

## Brief Answer

There are strong grounds to suppress. The frisk exceeded its lawful scope, the ensuing search was not justified by any recognized warrant exception, and the statements followed directly from the unlawful seizure.

## Discussion

### 1. The frisk exceeded the narrow authority of Terry.

A protective frisk is permissible only on reasonable suspicion that the suspect is armed, and it is limited to a patdown for weapons. *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), authorized a limited search of the outer clothing for weapons, not an evidentiary search. Here the officer reached into the defendant's pockets and removed a closed container, which exceeds what *Terry* permits.

### 2. The warrantless search was not a valid search incident to arrest.

A search incident to a lawful arrest is confined to the arrestee's person and the area within his immediate control. *Chimel v. California*, 395 U.S. 752 (1969), rejected searches of an entire residence as incident to an arrest, limiting the exception to the arrestee's "wingspan." The defendant also retained a reasonable expectation of privacy in the closed container; under *Katz v. United States*, 389 U.S. 347 (1967), what a person "seeks to preserve as private" is constitutionally protected. The State responds that the search of the entire apartment was lawful as incident to the arrest, citing *Brown v. Mississippi*, 297 U.S. 278 (1936), for the proposition that a search incident to arrest may extend to the whole premises. That reliance is misplaced.

### 3. The exclusionary rule requires suppression of the evidence and its fruits.

Illegally obtained evidence is inadmissible. *Mapp v. Ohio*, 367 U.S. 643 (1961), applied the exclusionary rule to the States, and *Weeks v. United States*, 232 U.S. 383 (1914), established it in federal prosecutions. Evidence derived from the initial illegality is likewise barred as fruit of the poisonous tree under *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471 (1963). The State separately argues that suppression of the physical evidence is unwarranted, but *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335 (1963), confirms that physical evidence seized without a warrant must be excluded, which cuts the other way.

### 4. The statements must be suppressed.

The defendant's statements followed a custodial interrogation conducted without warnings, in violation of *Miranda v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 436 (1966). Independently, *Delgado v. United States*, 593 U.S. 401 (2021), holds that statements obtained after an unlawful stop are presumptively involuntary and must be suppressed absent attenuation.

## Conclusion

We should move to suppress the container and its contents under *Terry*, *Chimel*, and *Katz*; the derivative evidence under *Wong Sun*; and the statements under *Miranda*.
