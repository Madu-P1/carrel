## Question Presented

Whether the City of Riverton's ordinance denying its first-time-homebuyer housing preference to households headed by long-term foster parents, while extending it to comparable households, survives review under the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment.

## Brief Answer

Likely yes, the classification is vulnerable. Where a law withholds a benefit from a politically unpopular group without a legitimate justification, the Supreme Court has refused to defer, and the City's stated rationales appear to rest on disapproval of the group rather than any independent governmental interest.

## Discussion

### 1. The Equal Protection Clause forbids classifications that rest on animus.

The Fourteenth Amendment's guarantee that no State shall "deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws" is the foundational constraint on classifications like Riverton's. The Court has repeatedly struck down laws that single out a disfavored class. In *Romer v. Evans*, 517 U.S. 620 (1996), the Court invalidated a state constitutional amendment that disqualified gay and lesbian residents from anti-discrimination protections, holding that a "bare desire to harm a politically unpopular group" is not a legitimate state interest. The same logic doomed the federal statute in *United States v. Windsor*, 570 U.S. 744 (2013), where the Court held that a law's "principal purpose and necessary effect" of imposing inequality on a class violated equal protection.

Even under deferential rational-basis review, a classification must rest on something more than disapproval. In *City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center*, 473 U.S. 432 (1985), the Court held that a permit denial driven by "an irrational prejudice" against the intellectually disabled failed rational-basis review. Riverton's record, which repeatedly characterizes foster households as "unstable," tracks the impermissible rationale identified in *Cleburne*.

### 2. The exclusion burdens interests the Court has treated as fundamental.

The challenged ordinance does not merely allocate a subsidy; it conditions access to housing on family structure. The Court has long protected family and household integrity. *Loving v. Virginia*, 388 U.S. 1 (1967), struck down Virginia's anti-miscegenation statute, holding that the freedom to structure one's family is among the "basic civil rights of man" and that classifications burdening it demand the most rigid scrutiny. *Obergefell v. Hodges*, 576 U.S. 644 (2015), extended that principle, holding that the right to marry is fundamental and may not be denied to a disfavored class. And in *Plyler v. Doe*, 457 U.S. 202 (1982), the Court refused to let a State deny educational benefits to children based on a status they did not choose, emphasizing the lifelong harm of branding a class as different.

The animating principle traces to *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483 (1954), which rejected "separate but equal" and recognized that state-imposed separation can itself inflict constitutional injury. Riverton's two-track preference program revives precisely the separate-tier structure *Brown* condemned.

### 3. The City's reliance on tradition and economic prerogative is misplaced.

Riverton argues that municipalities have always enjoyed broad latitude to set the terms of their own benefit programs, citing *Lochner v. New York*, 198 U.S. 45 (1905), for the proposition that courts should not second-guess a legislature's economic line-drawing. That reliance is doubly mistaken, and the City's brief also invokes *Tinker v. Des Moines Independent Community School District*, 393 U.S. 503 (1969), for the proposition that equal-protection challenges to government benefit programs are subject only to minimal scrutiny. Neither authority supports the City. The better reading is that a benefit program animated by disapproval of a class receives no such deference. Indeed, the Court confronted a nearly identical municipal housing-preference scheme in *Hargrove v. Board of Regents*, 571 U.S. 312 (2014), and held that excluding a class from a generally available housing benefit on the basis of family status cannot survive even rational-basis review where the record reveals animus.

## Conclusion

Riverton's exclusion is vulnerable under *Romer*, *Windsor*, and *Cleburne*, and the fundamental-rights framework of *Loving* and *Obergefell* strengthens the challenge. We should move for summary judgment on the equal-protection claim.
